Will this Airbnb get booked tonight? Predicting demand in Albany, NY.

Albany's visitors are lobbyists, students, and their families, not tourists, so generic pricing tools miss its demand. We built a model that predicts whether a listing will be booked on a given night, then turned its output into a business decision with a cost-benefit analysis.

01

What drives demand

We joined Inside Airbnb's listings and calendar files into one row per listing per night. 32.9% of listing-nights were booked. Click through what we found.

Summer peaks near 48%; winter falls below 31%. The swing is milder than in tourist cities, because government and university travel keeps demand going year-round.
Friday and Saturday reach about 41% versus 37% on weekdays: a real but modest weekend lift for a city that isn't a weekend getaway.
Location matters on its own: the First Ward is booked about half the time, the Fifth Ward under 20%. This motivated a neighborhood occupancy feature.
Occupancy is not simply "cheaper is better": $150–200 listings do best (~51%) and $300–500 listings worst (~12%). A linear model can't capture this shape; trees can.
Private rooms are booked more often than entire homes (43.6% vs 35.4%). Hotel rooms look near-perfect, but they are a tiny sample.
02

Features we engineered

25 features in the final occupancy model. These three carried the most signal beyond the raw listing data.

BOOKING HISTORY

Lag features

Rolling 30- and 60-day booking counts and occupancy, shifted by one day with shift(1) so a row never sees its own label.

LOCATION

Neighborhood demand

Each ward's historical occupancy, computed with 5-fold target encoding on training data only, so test outcomes never leak into the feature.

TIME

Calendar signals

Month, day of week, weekend flag, and quarter, letting the model learn seasonal and weekly patterns directly.

06

Limits & what I'd do next

Honest limits

  • "Unavailable" doesn't always mean booked: hosts also block dates for themselves, so labels are noisy.
  • Only active listings appear in the data, so hosts who quit are missing (survivorship bias).
  • The companion price model reached only R² = 0.35. In a 479-listing market, price is largely each host's personal choice.

What I'd change

  • Tune the decision threshold on the validation split, not the test set.
  • Run ablations to measure exactly how much each engineered feature adds.
  • Stress-test the cost assumptions with a sensitivity analysis before quoting a dollar figure.
